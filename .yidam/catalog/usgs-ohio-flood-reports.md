**What an indirect measurement is, and why it is not a gauge reading.** Where no gauge stood, USGS
surveys the marks the water left — silt lines, debris, stains inside buildings — levels them against
a datum, and computes the discharge from channel geometry and a uniform-flow or energy equation. The
report names the method for each site: slope-area, contracted-opening, Manning's equation, or a
routing model. **The result is a measurement of that flood and not a reading of an instrument**, and
it exists only because somebody went out afterwards and looked. A county with no gauge is not
therefore a county with no measurement.

**Its Allen County row.** Bluffton, on Riley Creek, from Bentley Road up to Interstate 75 — 2.4
stream miles and 30 high-water marks; 35.8 square miles of drainage; slope-area method; **10,300
cubic feet a second**, at an estimated recurrence interval **greater than 500 years**. It is the
only Allen County site in the report, and 421 marks were surveyed across the nine communities in
all.

**A recurrence interval is a statistical statement and not a promise.** "Greater than 500 years"
means the flow lies past the far end of the frequency curve fitted to the region's gauges. It does
not mean five centuries will pass before the next one, and this report puts seven of its eight
ungaged sites in that band on a single day.

**Its 1959 pair reports what the annual-peak file cannot.** Ohio flooded twice in three weeks in
1959 — late January and 10–11 February — and both fall in water year 1959, so the peak service
publishes one row and the larger of the two. The station tables in these reports give both. On the
Ottawa at Allentown the January flood was the larger; the February one reached 5,150 cubic feet a
second, which the annual series never shows.

**Its older discharges may be stages with the discharge undetermined.** WSP 1750-A records the March
1939 flood at Allentown as "a stage of 10.1 ft" and the May 1943 flood as "about 10 ft (discharge
not determined)". The peak service now carries 6,160 and 6,000 cubic feet a second against those
dates, flagged as historic peaks and estimates. The numbers came later than the water.
